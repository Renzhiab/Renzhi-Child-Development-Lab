# Recent Paper Highlights

## 1. Not All Defending Is Equal: How Bystanders' Strategies Shape Their Own Adjustment

When children witness bullying, some intervene—but they do not all intervene in the same way. In a three-wave longitudinal study of 1,259 Chinese children (mean age 10.9 years), we used latent transition analysis to examine how young people combine different defending strategies over time. Four profiles emerged: aggressive and non-aggressive defenders, average defenders, infrequent defenders, and non-aggressive defenders. Non-aggressive and average defenders were relatively stable, whereas the other profiles changed more across waves. Staying in, or moving toward, the non-aggressive defender profile was linked to better social-emotional adjustment, while profiles involving aggression carried greater social risk. The findings suggest that anti-bullying programs should do more than encourage bystanders to act: they should help them defend safely and constructively.

**Reference:** Jin, G., Wei, L., Wang, Z., Ren, H., & Liu, J. (2026). Defending victimized peers in unique ways: Profiles and transitions in defending strategies and their associations with youth social-emotional adjustment. *Journal of School Psychology, 115*, 101524. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2025.101524

**Corresponding authors:** Huiguang Ren; Junsheng Liu

## 2. Measuring How Young People Relate to AI—Beyond Convenience

As generative AI becomes part of everyday academic, work, and social life, young adults increasingly turn to it for emotional disclosure and companionship, not just for tasks. Research has lagged behind because there were few validated tools for capturing these experiences. Using a regionally representative sample of 1,406 college students from 26 universities in Shanghai, we developed and validated a three-scale toolkit: the Multidimensional AI Use Scale, the Perceived Uniqueness of AI Interaction Scale, and the AI Emotional Attachment Scale. Split-sample exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses supported clear factor structures, good reliability, and discriminant and criterion-related validity. The toolkit captures three layers of human–AI engagement—behavioral use, cognitive perceptions, and affective attachment—providing a rigorous foundation for studying why some young people form deep bonds with AI and what those bonds mean for their development.

**Reference:** Chen, C., Ren, H., Liu, J., Tian, F., & Wang, F. (2026). A three-layered framework and measurement toolkit for understanding emotional and relational engagement with AI. *International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2026.2725999

**Corresponding author:** Huiguang Ren

## 3. What Parents Say After a Failure Can Shape Adolescent Burnout

When adolescents do poorly on an exam, parents' immediate response can carry lasting weight. Using three waves of data from 806 Chinese parent–adolescent dyads (adolescents' mean age 13.9 years, Grades 7–8), we examined two forms of parental failure socialization: process-oriented messages that emphasize effort and strategies, and person-oriented messages that emphasize ability or the child as a person. Process-oriented socialization predicted a stronger failure-is-enhancing mindset, which in turn predicted lower school burnout; person-oriented socialization showed the opposite pattern. Adolescents' learning orientations amplified these effects: cognitive persistence strengthened the benefits of process-focused messages, whereas negative reactions to failure intensified the costs of person-focused ones. The results point to a practical lever for families—how failure is framed at home shapes how adolescents interpret setbacks and how burned out they feel at school.

**Reference:** Chen, C., Ren, H., Zhou, T., & Liu, J. (2026). Parental socialization of academic failure and adolescent school burnout: The mediating role of failure mindset and moderating role of learning orientations. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-026-02411-z

**Corresponding authors:** Huiguang Ren; Junsheng Liu
